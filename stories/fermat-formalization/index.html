The work after Fermat’s Last Theorem was proved

Fermat’s Last Theorem already had a human proof. Claude agents helped turn it into a computer-checked one by building, repairing and connecting thousands of intermediate arguments.

By Sander Knape

Draft preview · Not yet published/5 min read

Fermat’s Last Theorem

xn+yn≠znx^n + y^n \ne z^nn>2n > 2

Some square numbers add up to another square: 3² + 4² = 5².

Fermat’s theorem says this never works for cubes or any higher whole-number power, when all three numbers are positive whole numbers.

On August 17, the last outstanding statement closed. According to Anthropic’s account of the project, its completion resolved the remaining dependencies within seconds, and the collaboration platform marked Fermat’s Last Theorem complete. The team still had to assemble and check the proof outside that platform.

The theorem says that no three positive whole numbers can satisfy an+bn=cna^n+b^n=c^n when the whole-number exponent nn is greater than two. Squares allow familiar examples such as 32+42=523^2+4^2=5^2. Cubes and higher powers don’t.

Mathematicians already had a proof, thanks to Andrew Wiles and Richard Taylor. What the agents helped produce was a formalization: an extensive translation of known mathematics into a language in which a computer could check the argument.

What I find interesting is that the theorem was already proved, yet making its proof checkable by a computer still left so much mathematical work to do.

A written proof leaves work for its reader

A mathematical proof is written for people who bring considerable knowledge to it. They recognize the objects under discussion, know which established results apply and can supply intermediate reasoning. A proof assistant such as Lean needs those objects defined and those connections formally justified.

Even getting to the point of stating a result can take substantial preparation. In his 2024 account of the human Fermat formalization project, mathematician Kevin Buzzard described attending number-theory seminars and finding that Lean lacked the definitions needed to state most weeks’ announced results. His project therefore involved building mathematical vocabulary as well as proving things. That describes the situation in 2024, rather than an inventory of what the later AI campaign inherited, but it makes the missing work tangible.

Anthropic reports that its agents worked through an exposition of the existing proof by Henri Darmon, Fred Diamond and Richard Taylor. They defined concepts, proved intermediate results and repaired formal code, building on human mathematics, Lean, its community-developed Mathlib library and earlier Fermat formalization projects.

The translation required mathematical problem-solving. An agent had to connect what an argument needed with what the formal library actually supplied, and establish the missing steps in a form Lean would accept.

Keeping connected arguments together

Anthropic attributes earlier unsuccessful attempts partly to agents losing track of project state and collaborating ineffectively. For the successful campaign, a Claude Code-based system coordinated dozens of agents, while a platform called Prove2Me tracked statements, proofs and their dependencies. Researcher Tianyi Peng occasionally supplied priorities.

The published Prove2Me design explains how work can proceed before every prerequisite is finished. As a teaching example, imagine one agent establishes that A and B together imply C. Other agents can work on A and B separately. The platform can check the connecting argument immediately, while keeping C unresolved until its prerequisites are proved.

The statements themselves stay fixed, so a proof of something slightly different cannot silently take the place of the result another worker needs. Searchable descriptions also help agents find existing mathematics before creating more.

These are coordination functions, with agents supplying the reasoning. Anthropic’s account identifies the infrastructure as important to the campaign, though it offers no controlled comparison measuring how much each component contributed.

Two episodes give a closer view of the agents’ work. Both come from Anthropic’s published timeline, whose excerpts were mostly selected by Claude and whose mathematical accounts were not independently audited.

In one, an agent challenged another agent’s assumption that denominators would remain bounded. The challenged agent recalculated and found increasing inverse powers: division by higher and higher powers of a number, defeating the fixed denominator bound its approach required. It abandoned that route. The statement it had been trying to prove remained unsettled; a failed argument did not establish that the statement was false.

Later, in a separate episode, an agent recognized that an earlier proof it had written already contained mathematics needed for another task. It used that work toward a new proof, which was accepted, and reported that another agent’s dependent statement subsequently closed.

The excerpt does not provide enough detail to reconstruct that mathematical derivation. It does show a useful sequence: the agent recognized an existing prerequisite, used it to complete a proof and thereby made a needed result available to work elsewhere in the project. No documented connection makes this the replacement for the abandoned denominator route.

Checking the argument - and what it proves

Once the final dependency closed, the team began checking the result outside the collaboration platform. It recompiled the components the next morning, assembled them into one Lean project the following day and ran further checks.

Two questions mattered. Did the formal argument satisfy the proof checker? And did the statement it proved actually express Fermat’s Last Theorem? A perfectly valid derivation of an accidentally weakened or altered statement would not complete the task.

The team’s checks addressed both proof validity and statement identity, including checking that the assembled proof had no unproved placeholders. Buzzard then personally compiled the code and successfully ran the statement comparator. Anthropic supplied his computing access, so this was a separate mathematician’s firsthand check using provided infrastructure.

That checking supports the completed-formalization claim. It does not independently validate every process episode in the timeline.

A complete proof, with a substantial footprint

Anthropic reports eleven days for the successful campaign, using dozens of agents powered by an internal research model. There is no scope-matched human timing or cost comparison, so the elapsed time cannot establish a measured human-versus-AI speedup.

The resulting artifact is enormous: about 13 million lines of Lean code. Its size and compilation burden also limit how conveniently others can work with it. Buzzard distinguishes this achievement from his own goals of producing reusable library contributions and a proof that humans can readily explore.

Making the output into a maintainable mathematical library remains separate work. What became available was already substantial: an existing human proof carried through thousands of connected formal obligations into a complete artifact whose argument a computer could check.
