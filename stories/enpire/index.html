Coding agents take on robot training

In ENPIRE, AI coding agents changed training software and tested it with real robots. Their work shows how agents can help improve physical skills, within a laboratory setup built and assessed by people.

By Sander Knape

Draft preview · Not yet published/4 min read

A robot learning to insert a pin can learn from human demonstrations: given what it sees, which action would match the example? It can also learn from scores that tell it whether its attempts succeeded. These give the training software different things to aim for. One favors the demonstrated behavior; the other favors actions expected to earn a better score.

In ENPIRE, coding agents experimented with combining the two. They changed the training so it kept encouraging the robot to imitate the demonstrations while also learning from rewards. That choice gets at a less visible part of robotics: someone has to decide how a robot should learn, write the software and find out whether it works.

Here, AI agents took on some of that engineering. The agents edited and ran training software. A separate learned program, called the robot’s policy, selected the robot’s physical actions. Training changed that policy; the study does not report retraining the coding agents themselves.

A lab the agents could work in

The researchers supplied the robots, human demonstrations, task requirements and assessment. They also brought together the underlying training software.

To run experiments, the agents needed ways to reset the robots, apply safety rules and check outcomes. They helped implement or refine those software interfaces. Once the interfaces were finalized, they stayed fixed while the agents worked on improving the policies. That let the agents change how a robot learned without also changing the rules used to score its attempts.

The resulting loop connected code to physical work. Agents could change the training software, run experiments, inspect feedback from the robots and use that information to decide what to try next. The researchers report that the agents needed little human intervention while improving the robots’ skills through these training experiments. Building and assessing the lab remained human work.

Choosing how the robot learns

Learning to copy demonstrations is usually called behavior cloning. Learning through rewards is called reinforcement learning. ENPIRE’s agents tried different combinations of these methods and adjusted the settings that governed training.

The agents could change how much weight the imitation goal received alongside learning from rewards.

The researchers report that pin-insertion success rates rose as the agents added imitation to reward-based training and made further adjustments. Several things changed during this work, so the results cannot tell us how much improvement any one change caused.

Those success measurements included recovery attempts after failures. They describe how well the system completed the task with opportunities to try again, rather than the chance of succeeding on its first attempt.

How do you score a zip tie?

Learning from success requires a way to recognize it. A separate ENPIRE task, threading a zip tie, makes that problem concrete.

As the project’s explanation shows, the checking software identifies the tie’s head and strap in camera images. It separates those image regions and combines judgments from two cameras about whether the strap passes through the head. The result becomes a simple success-or-failure reward for training.

The coding agents worked on this detector too. They tried different prompts for the image model and adjusted which parts of the images it examined. They also adapted the detector’s software to meet the lab’s speed requirement. The authors report that its processing took less than 150 milliseconds; that is detector processing time, not the robot’s total reaction time.

In their description of developing the zip-tie detector, the researchers do not say how often it judged the result correctly. Keeping a scoring rule fixed helps make comparisons meaningful, but it cannot guarantee that the rule recognizes every physical outcome correctly.

The zip-tie detector and the pin-training changes are separate examples of the engineering involved. One makes physical attempts usable as feedback; the other changes how the robot learns from experience.

Passing on the training experience

After the pin-insertion work, the researchers asked the agents to write down what they had learned about training. They then added that written guidance to the instructions for a new task: inserting a GPU, a processor used for graphics and AI computing.

The next run did not inherit the earlier robot’s recorded attempts, hidden logs or saved trained policy. The connection was the written summary.

The researchers report high success on the new task, again with recovery attempts included, though their experiments do not show how much the written guidance helped. The agents’ pin-insertion work had become part of the instructions for training on a different physical task.
